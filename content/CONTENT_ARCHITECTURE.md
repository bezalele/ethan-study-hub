# Ethan Study Hub — Content & Learning Architecture

**Owner:** ChatGPT (lead engineer + content lead)  
**Audience:** Ethan (14, freshman) + parent learning alongside him  
**North star:** Inspire curiosity first; quietly guarantee AP U.S. Government coverage underneath.

## Editorial voice
Write like a superb museum guide or documentary narrator, not a textbook.
- Begin with a human question or problem.
- Explain cause → consequence → next question.
- Short paragraphs.
- Plain language first; precise vocabulary second.
- Make the reader want to know what happens next.
- Separate established fact from interpretation/debate.
- Political material remains nonpartisan and evidence-based.
- Deep detail is optional/on demand.

## The Founding Journey — first content spine

### Scene 0 — Why government at all?
**Hook:** Put a group of people together and practical questions appear almost immediately: Who makes shared rules? Who settles disputes? Who protects the community? Who pays for common needs? And who controls the people given power?

**Big idea:** Government is one way societies create institutions with authority to make and enforce collective decisions. The American story adds a second question: how can that authority be strong enough to work while still constrained?

**Discover prompts:**
- What happens if nobody can enforce a rule?
- What happens if one person can make every rule and cannot be challenged?
- Can a government be both strong and limited?

**Vocabulary on demand:** authority, legitimacy, rule of law, natural rights, social contract, consent of the governed.

### Scene 1 — Before there was a United States
**Narrative:** The future United States began as separate British colonies along the Atlantic coast, with their own local political institutions but under British imperial authority. Do not present 1607 as "the beginning of America"; Indigenous peoples and societies long predated European colonization.

**Question forward:** If colonists were accustomed to some local self-government, what happens when they believe distant imperial decisions violate their rights?

### Scene 2 — From dispute to independence
Present the escalating political dispute as context, not a full AP U.S. History detour.

**Destination:** 1776.

### Scene 3 — The Declaration: "Here is why we are leaving"
**Mental model:** The Declaration is not the Constitution. It announces separation and makes an argument for it.

Progressively reveal:
1. ideals/principles — equality, rights, government based on consent
2. grievances — the argument that British rule violated those principles
3. conclusion — independence

**Connection:** Declaring independence answers "Why leave?" It does not answer "How will the new country govern itself?"

### Scene 4 — You won independence. Now what?
Make this a dramatic transition.

**Prompt:** Thirteen states have rejected a powerful distant government. Would they immediately build another powerful central government?

### Scene 5 — Articles of Confederation: the first design
**Mental model:** A union of states with a deliberately limited central government.

Show visually:
- States: substantial retained authority
- Confederation Congress: national decision-making body
- no separate national executive comparable to the later presidency
- no national judiciary comparable to the later federal court system
- Congress could request money from states rather than levy taxes directly
- important national actions could be difficult because the system depended heavily on state cooperation

Avoid the lazy formulation "the Articles did nothing." Explain what the Confederation could do and why its limits mattered.

### Scene 6 — When the design meets reality
Organize around problems, not memorization:
- revenue/debt
- interstate and foreign commerce
- enforcement/coordination
- amendment/unanimity constraints
- interstate disagreements
- unrest such as Shays' Rebellion as part of the broader debate over governmental capacity

**Question forward:** Can the system be repaired, or does the country need a different design?

### Scene 7 — Philadelphia, 1787
The Constitutional Convention becomes a design problem.

Core tensions to visualize:
- national power ↔ state power
- large states ↔ small states
- effective government ↔ fear of concentrated power
- majority rule ↔ protections against abuse
- slavery was embedded in major constitutional compromises; do not sanitize this history

### Scene 8 — A new architecture
Introduce:
- popular sovereignty
- separation of powers
- checks and balances
- federalism
- enumerated powers
- representative government
- amendment process

Then transition into the Constitution Explorer.

### Scene 9 — Ratification: the argument was not over
Federalists and Anti-Federalists debated whether the proposed Constitution created the right balance of national power and liberty.

Use Federalist and Anti-Federalist writings as competing arguments to inspect, not as teams the site tells Ethan to join.

### Scene 10 — Bill of Rights
Explain why the first ten amendments became part of the constitutional settlement and then allow zoom into each right.

### Scene 11 — The constitutional story keeps changing
The founding is not the end. Use later amendments, civil rights struggles, institutional development, Supreme Court interpretation, elections and political participation to branch naturally into the rest of AP Government.

## Constitution Explorer content map

### Preamble
High level: states the Constitution's purposes; it is the doorway into the document.

### Article I — Legislative
Congress. Zoom: House → Senate → legislative powers → limits/process.

### Article II — Executive
President/executive. Zoom: qualifications/election framework → powers → duties → impeachment/removal framework.

### Article III — Judicial
Federal judiciary. Zoom: Supreme Court/federal courts → judicial power/jurisdiction → treason provisions. Judicial review should be taught with Marbury v. Madison, not falsely described as an explicit phrase in Article III.

### Article IV — States
Relations among states; obligations among states; admission of new states; federal guarantees.

### Article V — Amendments
How the Constitution can be formally changed.

### Article VI — Supremacy and obligations
Debts, Supremacy Clause, oaths, no religious test for federal/public trust under the United States.

### Article VII — Ratification
How the Constitution would take effect.

### Amendments
- 1–10: Bill of Rights
- 11–27: later constitutional changes
Give each amendment a one-line mental model, then zoom for exact language/history/application.

## Required AP coverage ledger — 2026–27
The discovery architecture must eventually map all five official AP units and skills without making the main experience feel like a syllabus.

College Board's Fall 2026 clarification adds four required foundational documents to the existing required set:
- Emancipation Proclamation
- Federalist No. 39
- Gettysburg Address
- Core Principles from Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations*

The content system must reserve first-class document experiences for all required foundational documents and required Supreme Court cases. Do not rely on an older nine-document list.

## Validation design
At the end of a discovery segment, use 2–5 lightweight checks:
- "Tell it back": explain the causal story in one or two sentences.
- Distinguish two commonly confused concepts/documents.
- One scenario/application.
- Later, one AP-style item where appropriate.

Progress states should be user-declared/validated, not inferred from page views.

## Source policy
Primary/current authorities first:
- College Board / AP Central for AP scope and exam alignment
- National Archives / Congress / Supreme Court / Library of Congress and other authoritative institutions for primary documents/history
- Curated reputable secondary sources when they add interpretation/context

Every image needs alt text and a source/credit. Prefer public-domain material when possible.

## Immediate content build order

3. Declaration / Articles / Constitution / Bill of Rights document explorers

5. AP coverage ledger mapping these experiences to official topics
